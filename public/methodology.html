Where the numbers come from

Data & method.

Read this first. PriceLens estimates the asking price in listings of used cars and bikes. It does not know completed sale prices, and the data comes from around 2020, so today's market can sit higher or lower. Use the range as a benchmark and a negotiation aid, not a guaranteed resale value.

Duplicates removed. Cars: rows with missing engine/power/seats and models with fewer than 5 listings are dropped. Bikes: models with fewer than 4 listings are dropped. The two checked-and-rejected leads: a public Indian smartphone price file that states all of its rows are synthetic, and a mobile/tablet file whose prices are normalised with no rupee basis.
Three LightGBM gradient-boosted models predict the 10th, 50th and 90th percentile of the log asking price. Inputs: make and model (target-encoded out-of-fold, so a model's own price never leaks into its training row), age, km driven, owners, seller type and, for cars, fuel, gearbox, engine size, power and seats. The "low end / fair / high end" figures are those percentiles, widened by a calibration step measured on held-out listings. The depreciation curve re-prices the same vehicle as it gets older and drives more. "What moved the price" re-prices the vehicle with one factor at a time set to a typical value.
  • Vehicles newer than model-year 2020 are refused, because nothing in the data covers them.
  • Models with too few listings are not in the picker. Anything that is not a car or bike (phones, laptops, appliances) is refused: there is no verified data for it yet.
  • No condition, accident, service history or city adjustment. The dataset does not contain them.
  • No sold-listing counts, no seasonal "best week to sell" and no live market feed. The data has no sale dates, so those would be invented.
  • Bikes have only about 750 usable rows, so their ranges are wider and less accurate than cars.
